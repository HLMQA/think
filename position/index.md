<h1 class="page-title">Make me think.</h1>
<p class="subtitle"><em>THINK Lab position statement</em></p>
</header>

<p>In 2000, Krug's <em>Don't Make Me Think</em> gave web design a simple yet gripping motto: interfaces should be so clear they never make people <em>think</em>. At the time, web design was at a different stage: conventions were few and far between, and non-technical users were often lost, having to translate their goals into foreign technical lingo. Against that backdrop, 'Don't make me think!' was akin to a fantasy, and served as an unofficial guiding principle for the following decades of web and software design. A quarter of a century later, we have largely overdelivered on this promise.</p>

<p>Today, AI-assisted decision making has been integrated in nearly all fields of professional and personal life. AI-first workflows suggest directions, draft texts, make plans, line up our next movie, summarise and reply to personal messages, and, all the while, offer a relentless succession of unsolicited next steps that users only need to validate and execute. This transition has brought about a sudden cliff in cognitive autonomy, making us the first generation able to delegate judgement itself, structurally and at scale, in almost every part of daily and professional identity. As comfortable as it is to defer judgement to an automated system, decades of research in neuropsychology also warn us that cognitive skills that go unused tend to shrivel. At the same time, experts, who are trusted to act as safeguards in human-in-the-loop systems, are vulnerable to cognitive biases and influences they are tasked to guard against.</p>

<p>At the THINK Lab, our vision is that we can no longer responsibly keep intentional mindlessness as a design goal. Instead, we must now turn our efforts to designing interfaces that help users retain cognitive autonomy and independent judgement, both so that we do not lose that ability, and so that we can live fully in our unique individual humanities. Three aspects of thinking motivate our approach:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Independence:</strong> the ability to form one's own judgement in the quiet intimacy of one's own reflective process, before - or without - the external influence of AI systems.</li>
  <li><strong>Confidence:</strong> the trust in one's own judgement and perception, with AI systems as potential but not required partners in moments of doubt or verification.</li>
  <li><strong>Humanity:</strong> the freedom and ability to think, not for the sake of efficiency or systematic accuracy, but because it is a manifestation of our free will, agency and humanity.</li>
</ul>

<p>The next paradigm is designing interfaces that, through thinking, allow us to remain architects of our individual and collective futures. The THINK Lab pursues this by investigating two parallel design paths:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Interfaces to <em>let</em> us think:</strong> interfaces and interactions that intentionally leave space and time for people to form their own judgement first, before introducing AI suggestions or nudging, and offer AI support only as a possible follow-up.</li>
  <li><strong>Interfaces to <em>make</em> us think:</strong> interfaces and interactions that intentionally trigger reflection in users. AI input is here intentionally absent, or when present, offers provocations, alternative readings, counterarguments or the evidence to weigh rather than conclusions.</li>
</ul>

<p>Just as we have spent the last decades creating the web of <em>'don't make me think'</em>, following the <em>'make me think'</em> approach will require equal amounts of collective effort. We invite researchers, designers, students and partners who share this concern to think with us.</p>
<p class="signature">Houda Lamqaddam, 4 November 2026</p>
